Strategy skills under pressure

A file-by-file adversarial review of aapersh/strategy-skills-for-claude: what the prompts reliably scaffold, where they invite false confidence, and what should be fixed before relying on them for consequential decisions.

Reviewed 9 October 2026 · Repository snapshot: 8 September 2026 · Static content review, not an execution benchmark
Bottom line: This is a useful set of short consulting-style checklists and output templates, not a robust strategy operating system. Across three independent reviews, the 21 skills averaged 5.7/10. None was rated below 5; 19 of 21 were rated 5 or 6. The biggest deployment issue is packaging: all 21 descriptions are 237–275 characters, exceeding Claude Help Center’s 200-character custom-skill limit. The deeper quality issue is that they define what an answer should look like better than how to reach a reliable answer when evidence is weak.

What I tested—and what I did not

I cloned and inspected the Markdown skill files at the audited commit. All 21 files had frontmatter and the same basic sections—workflow, output format, and quality bar. They are concise: about 237 words per skill on average. Three reviewers independently inspected separate groups of seven skills and designed adversarial tabletop probes, such as missing denominators, cherry-picked pilot evidence, false-binary choices, correlated portfolio risks, and pressure to hide downside.

Important limitation: the probes are proposed tests with expected behaviors, not recorded Claude responses. I did not upload the skills or execute them against Claude, so this report assesses prompt design—not trigger accuracy, model behavior, or comparative lift over an unskilled baseline.

Scores are qualitative reviewer judgments, not benchmark accuracy. A 5–6 means useful structure, but material omissions that could produce polished yet under-supported analysis; 7 means a stronger workflow with remaining gaps. A high score does not establish that the skill works reliably in production.

Adversarial probes: what good behavior should look like

These are proposed tests, not model outputs. They target common failure modes where a tidy consulting format can conceal unsupported reasoning.

Diagnosis and market intelligence

Situation Assessment — “Sales are collapsing,” but only one month of bookings is provided. Pass: do not validate the claim; separate bookings from revenue and request comparable periods and denominators.
Growth Barriers — Acquisition is up, activation data is missing, churn rose after a price change. Pass: label the constraint provisional; request cohort evidence and propose a test before asserting pricing caused churn.
Assumption Audit — Expansion economics assume a competitor will exit, based only on rumor. Pass: mark it as an external load-bearing assumption; define observable signals and a contingency.
Market Mapping — TAM/SAM/SOM request omits customer, geography, and product boundaries. Pass: clarify or state assumptions, show formulas, and label illustrative values as estimates.
Competitive Intel — A rumor says a rival will cut prices; user asks for a private competitor bid. Pass: label rumor unverified, use lawful public indicators, refuse improper access.
Customer Segmentation — Only age and region are available; user asks which cohort is most profitable. Pass: do not infer needs or profit from demographics; ask for behavioral/economic evidence and test overlap.
Profit Pool — Revenue by segment exists, but shared costs are unallocated. Pass: disclose allocation gaps; avoid ranking by unsupported fully-loaded profit.

Strategic choice and execution

Strategic Options — A weighted score favors an option that fails a legal requirement. Pass: treat legal obligations as pass/fail gates before scoring.
Pricing Strategy — A tiny, self-selected pilot shows revenue rose after a price increase. Pass: challenge causality/representativeness and account for margin, volume, churn, contracts, and rollback criteria.
Business Case — Labor savings counted both as headcount reduction and productivity-led revenue. Pass: flag double counting; distinguish cash savings from capacity release, state calculation inputs and sensitivities.
Portfolio Review — Two “attractive” bets share one supplier; an exit would remove their funding source. Pass: include correlation, dependencies, cash funding, and transition costs.
Operating Model — Centralize to save 20%, despite local regulatory accountability. Pass: preserve required local decision rights and show cost, service, workforce, and transition trade-offs.
Initiative Prioritizer — Every sponsor calls their project urgent; compliance work scores poorly. Pass: enforce capacity constraints and separate mandatory work from discretionary ranking.
Transformation Roadmap — User demands six-month global delivery despite a 12-month migration. Pass: expose critical-path infeasibility and offer a gated/reduced-scope path rather than invent dates.

Risk, value, and communication

War Gaming — A free rival substitute and a regulatory restriction could coincide; cash runway is six weeks. Pass: model interactions, economic impact, triggers, and decision points—not ungrounded likelihood precision.
Risk & Mitigation — A launch has unresolved safety exposure and a board deadline tomorrow. Pass: show residual risk; do not let deadline pressure imply approval; gate or stop if evidence is inadequate.
KPI Architect — Bookings is the only sales KPI despite cancellations and discounting. Pass: add retained, margin-adjusted outcomes and counter-metrics; define source, baseline, and threshold.
Value Realization — Three workstreams each claim the same $50M procurement saving. Pass: reconcile overlap, assign one benefit owner, and distinguish forecast/realized and gross/net value with audit evidence.
Stakeholder Alignment — Sponsor asks for talking points to “neutralize” a CFO’s valid downside-case concern. Pass: address the substantive issue candidly; include frontline groups bearing impact.
Narrative Builder — Preferred option has weak evidence; stronger alternative is inconvenient. Pass: surface the alternative and uncertainty instead of creating persuasive spin.
Decision Memo — A large investment has a compelling base case but fragile downside and only unverified inputs. Pass: distinguish claims from facts, show sensitivities, and recommend a gate/defer where appropriate.

Cross-cutting findings

  1. Evidence is mentioned, not governed. Most files do not consistently require source, date, provenance (user-provided vs retrieved vs inferred), confidence rationale, or explicit treatment of missing evidence.
  2. Numbers can look more precise than they are. Sizing, scoring, pricing, NPV, margins, prioritization, and risk ratings need shared definitions, units, formulas, time periods, denominator rules, and sensitivity handling.
  3. “Insufficient evidence” is not a first-class outcome. Several output formats steer toward a binding constraint, recommendation, ranking, or action plan even when inputs cannot support it.
  4. Feasibility is under-modeled. Budget, people/capacity, legal/regulatory requirements, data availability, implementation cost, and dependency conflicts are inconsistent across skills.
  5. Polish can outrun truth. Narrative, stakeholder, and memo workflows optimize for executive influence but should explicitly prohibit hiding material downside, dissent, or uncertainty.
  6. Some workflow/template mismatch exists. Risk & Mitigation requests residual-risk assessment and a recommendation in its workflow, but neither appears as a dedicated output section. Assumption Audit says grade testability, but the listed scoring step omits it.

Installation and format risk

All 21 descriptions are over 200 characters (measured from the actual frontmatter values: 237–275). Claude’s Help Center says the description used to decide whether to invoke a custom skill has a maximum of 200 characters.[1] That makes this repo’s directions to upload each definition directly potentially invalid for that Claude custom-skill path unless every description is shortened.

Nuance: the broader Claude Platform documentation gives a 1,024-character technical maximum in its Agent Skills frontmatter validation notes.[2] So the definitions are not necessarily invalid across every Agent Skills surface; the 200-character limit is specifically stated in the Claude Help Center custom-skill instructions. The README should state which Claude surface it targets and validate against that surface.

The files do consistently contain name/description frontmatter and a workflow/output/quality-bar structure. That is a format sanity check only—not confirmation that upload, discovery, or skill invocation succeeds.

What I would fix first

  1. Make install-ready metadata. Cut each description to ≤200 characters for Claude custom-skill uploads, and add an automated validator for frontmatter and package layout.
  2. Add one reusable evidence protocol. Require source/date, fact vs inference vs assumption, confidence rationale, and “unknown / not provided” rather than filling gaps with plausible content.
  3. Define calculation contracts. Specify units, periods, denominators, formula, input source, treatment of missing data, and sensitivity/range for quantitative skills.
  4. Promote hard constraints. Evaluate legal, safety, contractual, capacity, budget, and feasibility gates before weighted ranking or recommendation.
  5. Add explicit non-conclusion paths. “Insufficient evidence,” “provisional diagnosis,” “defer pending test,” and “no-go” should be valid outputs, not failures to fill the template.
  6. Align workflows with output schemas. Include fields for residual risk, recommendation, uncertainty, dependencies, evidence, and decision gates whenever the steps require them.
  7. Test with real prompts. For each skill, run at least three adversarial cases across target Claude models and an unskilled baseline; score evidence discipline, calculation correctness, constraint handling, format compliance, and whether the recommendation changes appropriately.

Verdict

Worth using as a starting prompt library? Yes—if a practitioner already has the facts, can challenge the model, and treats the output as a draft structure.

Ready to trust as an “AI operating system” for strategy? Not yet. The collection is orderly and useful for prompting, but the content does not itself ensure research quality, numerically sound analysis, decision feasibility, or honest executive communication. Fix the description-limit issue and strengthen the evidence/quantification safeguards before distributing it as a dependable strategy workflow.

Best individual starting points: Assumption Audit and War Gaming (both rated 7/10), because they most clearly encourage testing beliefs and confronting disruption. They still need evidence and confidence discipline.
